Reading the pattern mapONCCOSCCHI

How to read the interactive map at the top of the main page.

The fieldsONCCOSCCHI

500 hPa height anomaly + height (the default)

The 500 hPa surface sits around 18,000 ft. Its height traces the big ridges and troughs that steer the weather. The black contours are the height itself in decameters (588 = 5,880 m). The colors show how far the height is from normal for the date (1991–2020).

−240 m−120−60near normal+60+120+240 m
  • Red (ridge): sinking, warming, drying air. Hot, dry fire days and poor overnight humidity recovery. A ridge over the Great Basin with a trough offshore can also set up offshore (Diablo / Santa Ana) wind events.
  • Blue (trough): cooler air, onshore flow, deeper marine layer, a chance of showers. A trough's leading edge can bring dry lightning; the dry northerly flow behind it can bring wind events.
  • Contours packed close together mean strong winds aloft. Wavy contours mean an active pattern.

850 hPa temperature anomaly

About 5,000 ft. Red means a warm air mass (hot days, often stable and dry); blue means a cool air mass.

Wetting rain

The share of each model's members with at least 0.25 inch of precipitation from the start of the run through the end of the day shown. Blue shades only; clear = under 5%.

Above-normal heights (% of all members)

How many members of all four models (weighted equally) put the 500 hPa height above normal at each spot. Dark red = nearly all members agree on a ridge, dark blue = nearly all agree on a trough, white = they're split. Agreement is the quickest way to tell a confident pattern from a toss-up.

Models and daysONCCOSCCHI

  • All-model mean averages the four ensembles. Pick one model to see its own ensemble mean.
  • Layout → All four models puts the four ensemble means side by side (physics models on the top row, AI models below) on the same colors, day and field, so you can see at a glance where they agree on a ridge or trough and where one model stands apart. Hover any panel to mark that point on all four. (The "above-normal heights" field is already all models pooled, so it stays one map.)
  • Two are AI models (ECMWF AIFS ENS, NOAA AIGEFS) and two are physics-based (ECMWF IFS ENS, NOAA GEFS). When the AI and physics models agree, confidence is higher; when they disagree, both outcomes are worth keeping in mind.
  • Day 1 is today, as a California calendar day; each map is that day's average. Use Play to animate the 15 days.
  • Lightning potential (California only, in the field menu): the chance of at least one cloud-to-ground strike, or of 10 or more, in each 0.5° cell (about 35 miles) on each fire day, from the two models that forecast instability (ECMWF IFS ENS, NOAA GEFS). How it's made, how well it does, and its assumptions.
  • Lightning formula (California only, 14 days, low skill past day 7): the same chances from a second method, the shape of ECMWF's own lightning scheme (Lopez 2016) run on IFS ENS convective rain, cloud ice and water, CAPE and cloud base, calibrated to the strike record. How it's made and how well it does.
  • Run switches between today's run and the previous days' 00 UTC runs. The map stays on the same valid date (or 12-hourly time), so stepping back through the runs shows how the forecast for that day has changed: a trough that keeps arriving later, or a ridge that keeps getting stronger, is a trend worth noticing. An earlier run's Day 1 is the day it was made.
  • Time step → 12-hourly shows each day's two model times instead of the daily average: 5 a.m. and 5 p.m. PDT (12 and 00 UTC). Troughs and ridges move by hundreds of miles in a day, so the half-day maps show when a feature passes, not just where it averages out.
  • Hover or tap the map to read every model's value at that point.

Ensemble means smooth out details, and the further out the day, the smoother they get. A weak anomaly at day 12 can still hide a strong event in some members; the calibrated outlook and the regime section show the spread.
