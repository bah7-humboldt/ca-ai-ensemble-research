Lightning potential: how it's made and its assumptionsONCCOSCCMethod 1 · logistic / CAPE

The "Lightning potential" fields in the pattern map's field menu.

What it showsONCCOSCC

For each 0.5° cell (about 35 miles across) over California and each fire day (6 a.m. to 6 a.m. Pacific), the chance that at least one cloud-to-ground lightning strike, or at least ten, lands in the cell. It's the average over the members of the two ensembles that forecast atmospheric instability, ECMWF IFS ENS and NOAA GEFS. The AI models don't output CAPE, so picking one of them, or the all-model mean, shows that two-model average. The colors are smoothed between cell centres for display; the forecast itself is per cell.

How it's madeONCCOSCC

It follows the method of Nikolov, Bothwell and Snook (2024), Probabilistic Forecasting of Lightning Strikes over the Continental USA and Alaska (Fire 7:111), which fits logistic equations to decades of observed strikes and reanalysis weather, with each area's lightning climatology built in. Their coefficients aren't published, so this is a re-fit on this project's data, not their model:

  • Strikes: Vaisala NLDN cloud-to-ground strikes, 2001–2025, counted per cell per fire day.
  • Weather: the ERA5 reanalysis at the same grid points and the models' two daily times (00 and 12 UTC, 5 p.m. and 5 a.m. PDT).
  • The equation's inputs, in order of weight:
    1. the cell's lightning frequency for that month (2001–2020), the baseline;
    2. instability, CAPE (the higher of the day's two values);
    3. 700 hPa (about 10,000 ft) dewpoint, moisture aloft for storms to build on;
    4. 2 m dewpoint, low-level moisture;
    5. 700 hPa temperature, and the 2 m minus 700 hPa temperature difference (how fast it cools with height) at 5 p.m.
  • Fitting: 2001–2020. Testing: 2021–2025, years the equation never saw.

Assumptions and limitsONCCOSCC

  • Trained on the reanalysis, run on forecasts. The equation learned from ERA5; the forecasts feed it model fields. Each model's CAPE is defined a little differently (IFS: most-unstable parcel; GEFS: a near-surface layer) and runs higher or lower than ERA5's, which shifts the probabilities. The project's run archive keeps each model's values so this can be measured and corrected.
  • Coarse instability. A 0.5° grid point stands for a 35-mile cell; the storms that make lightning are a few miles across. The model can tell which areas and days are favorable, not which canyon gets hit.
  • California only. The strike record covers California's Predictive Service Areas, so cells elsewhere have no equation.
  • Fire days, not hours. Each value is for a whole 6 a.m.–6 a.m. day. Night-time elevated storms (like August 2020's) count toward the day they start in.
  • Rare events run low. The climatology term pulls unusual events (early-season lightning) toward the usual, so a siege can show as 10–20% rather than 50%. See the June 2008 example below.
  • No lifting term. CAPE and moisture say storms can grow; nothing in the equation says what sets them off. See the false alarms and watch-outs below.
  • Dry or wet isn't in it. It forecasts strikes, not whether they come with rain. The "Thunderstorms and new lightning fires" rows in the calibrated outlook combine the storm setup with fuels and the record of lightning-caused fires.
  • Licensed data. The strike history is Vaisala NLDN, used under license; only derived probabilities and maps are shown.

Past sieges, as the model sees themONCCOSCC

The equation run on the reanalysis for five of the biggest lightning days of 2001–2025. Dots are the cells that had strikes, bigger for more. 2025 is a year the equation never saw. On every one of these days more cells were hit than the equation expected: on the big outbreaks it gets the area right and undersells the size. June 2008 is the worst case. It was an early-season siege where lightning almost never happens, so the equation's climatology term held it to 10–20% and not one cell reached 30%. Read the layer as "where storms can happen" more than "which cells will be hit", and treat any organized 10–20% area in June as worth a look.

Watch-outs: ingredients that can leave a high chance emptyONCCOSCC

Across every cell-day of 2001–2025 the equation put at 30% or more, about half had no strike. That's roughly what a 30–50% forecast should do. Which ones stayed empty wasn't random, though:

  • Warm air near 10,000 ft (a cap). In the warmest quarter of these cell-days (700 hPa above about 12.7 °C) 65% stayed dry, against 36% in the coolest quarter. On three of the four false alarms the 700 hPa temperature was 12–13 °C, against 9–11 °C on most of the sieges. Warm air aloft is a lid that CAPE may not get through without strong lift. The exception is elevated storms: on August 16, 2020 it was 16.6 °C at 700 hPa and storms fired anyway, on moisture and lift arriving above that layer.
  • A very hot surface under a deep dry layer. The miss rate climbs from 34% to 73% from the smallest to the largest quarter of the 2 m − 700 hPa temperature difference (above about 25 °C). That's desert afternoons: high cloud bases, storms that form over the mountains and drift or die before they reach the cell, or virga.
  • Moisture at the surface only. Three of the false alarms had 2 m dewpoints of 15.4–17.5 °C, against 12–13 °C on most of the sieges, and were no wetter than the sieges at 700 hPa. That's a Gulf of California moisture surge into the low deserts. It puts big CAPE on paper for a surface parcel (1,100 J/kg on August 22, 2009), but the moisture is shallow and capped. Look for moisture through the mid-levels, not just at the surface.
  • No trigger. The equation has no lifting term: no shortwave trough, no upper-level divergence, no outflow from yesterday's storms. CAPE is fuel, not ignition. Before trusting a high chance, look for something to set it off: an easterly wave or monsoon impulse, a closed low or shortwave approaching, or difluent flow aloft.
  • Things the equation can't see. Inhibition (CIN) isn't an input, and neither is mid-level moisture above 700 hPa or the steering flow. Heavy smoke can also shade the surface enough to keep afternoon storms from firing.
  • Early season. High chances in March and April came true least often (66% and 83% of cell-days at ≥30% stayed dry), and June the most of the main months (26% dry).
  • CAPE alone is a poor guide. Among cell-days at ≥30%, the miss rate is about the same (48–55%) whatever the CAPE. The forecast already counts CAPE; what decides the outcome is whether the air releases it.
