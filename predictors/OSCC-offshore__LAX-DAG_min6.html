LAX–DAG surface pressure gradient

Calibrated predictor · Southern California, offshore season (Oct, Nov, Dec, Jan) · footprint: Central and South Coast and the Southern California mountains (Santa Ana) · today's run 2026-10-17 10:25 PT

What it measures

Sea-level pressure at Los Angeles International Airport minus Daggett-Barstow, in hPa, taken as the lowest (most negative) value of the fire day at the 00, 06, 12 and 18 UTC observations. Negative means pressure is higher inland than at the coast, which pushes air from the interior toward the coast: offshore flow.

Why it matters for fire

Santa Ana (LAX-Daggett). Offshore winds are dry (the air sinks and warms as it comes down off the mountains), gusty, and blow toward the populated coastal hills. Strong offshore gradients go with the fastest-spreading fires in California's record: Diablo and North winds in the north, Santa Ana winds in the south.

Stations

  • LAX: Los Angeles International Airport (33.94°N, 118.41°W)
  • DAG: Daggett-Barstow (34.85°N, 116.79°W)

Arrow: the direction the pressure difference pushes air (from high to low pressure) when the gradient is negative.

Where fire grew: the satellite footprint

This measure is scored against fire growth inside its footprint: Central and South Coast and the Southern California mountains (Santa Ana), PSAs SC07 Central Coast, SC08 South Coast, SC09 Western Mountains, SC10 Eastern Mountains, SC11 Southern Mountains. Only acres that satellites saw burning inside those PSAs count toward its big growth days.

On a typical offshore season day, 1.5% of days have at least 5,000 acres of satellite-mapped fire growth in Central and South Coast and the Southern California mountains (Santa Ana). When this measure is past the elevated cutoff, such days are about 2.0 times as likely as usual for the same time of year. The cutoff is the most inclusive one that reaches ×2 with at least 25 days of history behind it.

The biggest growth days and what this measure showed

Central and South Coast and the Southern California mountains (Santa Ana), offshore season, 2001–present. Acres are only those inside the footprint; fire names and acres from satellite detections inside mapped perimeters. 38 of 47 days with ≥5,000 acres were past the elevated cutoff ("no obs": a station didn't report that day).

The five biggest days

  • Sun Oct 26, 2003: LAX–DAG surface pressure gradient was -5.8 hPa (past the high cutoff). In SC08 South Coast, the CEDAR ran 20 miles and grew 152,345 acres that fire day (307,834 acres across the footprint).
  • Sun Oct 21, 2007: LAX–DAG surface pressure gradient was -9.9 hPa (past the high cutoff). With ERC at the 67th percentile (33) in SC08 South Coast, the WITCH ran 23 miles and grew 73,136 acres that fire day (185,542 acres across the footprint).
  • Mon Oct 22, 2007: LAX–DAG surface pressure gradient was -10.0 hPa (past the high cutoff). With ERC at the 82nd percentile (38) in SC08 South Coast, the WITCH ran 12 miles and grew 83,697 acres that fire day (169,796 acres across the footprint).
  • Sat Oct 25, 2003: LAX–DAG surface pressure gradient was -5.5 hPa (past the high cutoff). In SC08 South Coast, the SIMI ran 20 miles and grew 53,810 acres that fire day (147,290 acres across the footprint).
  • Mon Oct 27, 2003: LAX–DAG surface pressure gradient was -3.5 hPa (past the elevated cutoff). In SC11 Southern Mountains, the CEDAR ran 5 miles and grew 33,537 acres that fire day (96,524 acres across the footprint).

New large fires

Growth isn't the only question: offshore and dry days also start the fires that become large. On the 1,540 offshore season days past the elevated cutoff, a fire that went on to burn at least 1,000 acres started in Central and South Coast and the Southern California mountains (Santa Ana) on 50 of them (3.2% of those days, against 1.7% on a typical offshore season day); about ×2.1 the usual odds for the date. That's 96% of the 52 large-fire starts there since 2001.
