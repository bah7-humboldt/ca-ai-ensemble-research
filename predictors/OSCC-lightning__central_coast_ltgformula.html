What it measuresOSCC

The number of cloud-to-ground lightning strikes the lightning formula expects across the footprint on the fire day. The formula follows the shape of ECMWF's own lightning scheme (Lopez 2016): convective rain, cloud ice and water, instability (CAPE) and cloud-base height, multiplied together, with its exponents and scale fit to 25 years of observed strikes. It replaced the plain CAPE measure this row used before.

Why it matters for fireOSCC

Lightning starts most of California's largest fires. In Northern California 235 of the 462 fires since 2001 that burned at least 1,000 acres were lightning-caused. Lightning fires come in clusters, often in remote country, and most are found on the day of the storm or the next. This row asks two things: will there be a lightning day, and how likely is a new large fire?

It was fit to observed strikes, Jun–Sep only (the formula's reanalysis history). On those days it ranks lightning days with an AUC of 0.94, against 0.91 for the CAPE setup it replaced. The row is still shown May–Oct: in May and October its cutoffs are carried over from summer, untested. Spring and fall storms are colder-based and lower-topped, so read those months with care. A full-year refit is planned.

A lightning day here is a fire day with at least 100 strikes inside the footprint's PSAs. On a typical Jun–Sep day 3.2% of days are lightning days; per year that's about May 0.4, Jun 0.4, Jul 1.0, Aug 1.2, Sep 1.4, Oct 0.9 by month. Past the elevated cutoff, 36% are, and 20% have 500 strikes or more. History 2001-2025 (Jun–Sep), 3,025 days, 91 past the cutoff.

New large lightning firesOSCC

On the 91 days past the cutoff, a lightning fire that went on to burn at least 1,000 acres was found in the footprint that day or the next on 3 of them (3.3%, against 0.4% on a typical Jun–Sep day): about ×7.5 the usual odds for the date. That covers 25% of the 12 days a large lightning fire was found there.
